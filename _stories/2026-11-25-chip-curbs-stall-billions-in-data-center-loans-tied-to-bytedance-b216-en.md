<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

Lenders have reportedly pulled back from billions of dollars in data-center loans tied to ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent, as US restrictions on AI chip exports complicate the financing, Bloomberg reported, citing people it did not name. Among the affected deals, some lenders are delaying a $6.6 billion loan for Bridge Data Centres, a Malaysia-headquartered data-center operator. The report describes a financing slowdown rather than any new export-control measure, and no lender or company named was quoted directly. The scale of the pullback beyond the Bridge loan, and whether any of the stalled deals have been cancelled outright, was not specified.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** Bloomberg reported that lenders have pulled back on billions of dollars in loans tied to ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent, and that some have delayed an upsized $6.6 billion loan sought by Bain Capital's Bridge Data Centres, whose Malaysian facilities serve clients including ByteDance; the account is attributed to people involved in the discussions.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The scale and cause of the pullback rest on unnamed people, not on statements from the lenders, Bain Capital, ByteDance, Alibaba or Tencent, and the report does not establish which lenders froze or delayed what, whether any deals were cancelled rather than paused, or how much of the total remains on hold.

</div>

## Background

The curbs in question are US restrictions on exports of advanced AI chips to China, which have led Chinese cloud and AI companies to seek compute through data centers outside the mainland. AI Daily Radar's November 15 digest, citing a Wall Street Journal investigation, reported that Nvidia chips had been reaching Chinese firms through large Southeast Asian data centers that exploited a gap in the sanctions; the same day's digest cited a New York Times report that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had pushed to narrow restrictions affecting a data center in Malaysia that may serve Tencent. The new reporting indicates that uncertainty over how those rules will be applied has now moved from chip procurement into the financing stage, where lenders are weighing offshore data-center projects against the risk that the tenants' access to chips could be cut off.

## Why it matters

For data-center developers and lenders in these deals, the constraint is shifting from buying chips to raising money: if banks and syndicates price in the risk that a site cannot be equipped with restricted US accelerators, loan commitments can slip or be repriced, leaving operators to fund construction from other sources. The practical concern for borrowers is timing — a delayed syndication such as the $6.6 billion Bridge Data Centres loan pushes back when capital actually reaches construction budgets.

## What to watch

Whether the $6.6 billion Bridge Data Centres loan syndication closes, and whether arrangers or the companies involved confirm the terms publicly rather than only through unnamed sources.
