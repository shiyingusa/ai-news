Malaysia plans to require data-center operators to disclose AI accelerator imports, power consumption, facility locations, and the identities of operators and hyperscale customers under a new law aimed at detecting GPUs diverted to export-restricted countries such as China. The Digital Ministry is reviewing existing legislation to determine whether authorities can act against re-exporters, and the proposal would also make operators pay for all electricity capacity they reserve, whether or not it is used; Malaysia has approved 117 data-center projects, 55 of them operational, according to the trade minister. The minister said authorities would cooperate with U.S. counterparts and provide information about facilities housing particular GPUs, but it is unspecified whether operators must report GPU models and serial numbers or whether Malaysia will conduct on-site inspections. The article notes that power data alone cannot reliably count accelerators because rated wattage varies—Nvidia's H100 and H200 SXM GPUs are rated at 700W, while B200 SXM is rated at 1,000W—and actual draw depends on workloads, server configuration, and facility power usage effectiveness.

</div>

## Background

Malaysia introduced export controls on advanced US-origin AI chips in 2025 following diplomatic pressure from Washington, and in June 2026 Malaysian authorities foiled an attempt to smuggle nearly $13 million in AI servers through Kuala Lumpur. The proposed disclosure law extends that enforcement approach from border seizures to the data-center level, where restricted accelerators can be imported legally and then diverted.

## Impact

For data-center operators in Malaysia, the plan would turn reserved electricity capacity into a paid commitment and add disclosure of facility locations and customer identities; until the law is finalized, it remains unclear whether they would also have to report GPU models and serial numbers or submit to physical inventory audits.
