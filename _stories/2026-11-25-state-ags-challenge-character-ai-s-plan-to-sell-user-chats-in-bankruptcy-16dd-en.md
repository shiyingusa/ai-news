A group of state attorneys general is challenging Character.AI's proposed bankruptcy sale of user data, saying the assets up for auction include sensitive personal information from more than 21 million U.S. accounts, about 2.1 million of which are registered to minors, TechCrunch reported. The objection targets the planned sale of the chatbot company's assets, in which that account data would change hands along with the rest of the estate. The AGs' challenge does not by itself stop the auction; it puts the privacy and child-safety questions in front of the bankruptcy court before any transfer is approved.

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** State attorneys general say the proposed Character.AI bankruptcy sale includes sensitive personal data from more than 21 million U.S. accounts, including 2.1 million registered to minors, TechCrunch reported.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The exact number of attorneys general involved (TechCrunch does not give one; a secondary report says 40), the sale price and winning bidder, and whether the sale will be blocked; Character.AI's claims about notifying users and redacting personal data rest on the company and a secondary report, and a single blog report that bids were due November 20 and Disney made a $20 million offer is not corroborated by TechCrunch.

</div>

## Background

In a U.S. bankruptcy, a company's assets can be sold to a buyer under court supervision, and a judge decides whether objections from creditors or regulators should block or reshape the deal — an objection window that state attorneys general routinely use when consumer data is among the assets on the block. Such objections typically hinge on whether a transfer of account and chat data conflicts with privacy commitments made to users or with state consumer-protection rules, including special protections for minors.

## Why it matters

For teams that build on user conversations, the objection shows that a bankruptcy asset sale is not a clean route for moving sensitive data: transferring 21 million accounts, including 2.1 million registered to minors, now draws state-level scrutiny that can delay a deal or attach conditions to it. Builders should treat retention limits, age gates and deletion commitments as liabilities that travel with the data, not as paperwork that stays behind with the seller.

## What to watch

Whether the bankruptcy court approves, delays or conditions the sale at its next hearing, and whether the identity of any buyer is disclosed.
