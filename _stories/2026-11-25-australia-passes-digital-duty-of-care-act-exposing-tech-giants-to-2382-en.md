Australia's Parliament passed the Digital Duty of Care Act on Wednesday, according to the Associated Press, creating an online safety obligation that could expose tech giants to multibillion-dollar fines if they fail to prevent foreseeable online harms. The law's core mechanism is a general duty of care rather than a list of banned content types, meaning platforms are expected to anticipate harm instead of responding only after specific material is reported. The AP report as excerpted does not give the maximum penalty figures, name the platforms covered, or state when the obligations take effect, so the practical reach of the regime is not yet established by the available reporting.

## Background

A duty of care is a legal standard borrowed from health and safety law: rather than regulators listing prohibited material and ordering takedowns after the fact, the regulated party must take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm and show it did so. Australia's measure was carried through Parliament as the Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026, so it amends the country's existing online safety legislation instead of replacing it with a standalone statute.

## Why it matters

Platforms serving Australian users would face liability for harms they could reasonably have foreseen, which shifts compliance work toward proactive risk assessment of product and moderation systems rather than post-hoc takedowns. Because the reported exposure runs into the billions, the operative questions for affected companies are the statutory penalty ceiling and the start date — both of which the excerpt leaves open.
