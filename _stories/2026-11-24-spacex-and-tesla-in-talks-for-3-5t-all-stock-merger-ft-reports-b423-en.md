The Financial Times reported, citing unnamed sources, that SpaceX and Tesla are negotiating an all-stock merger valued at $3.5 trillion, a combination that would be the largest merger on record if completed. The talks are being overseen by two special committees—board groups meant to handle conflicts—of five directors described as "disinterested," all of whom have ties to Elon Musk, according to the report.

The all-stock structure would pay shareholders in shares rather than cash. The report gives no timeline or regulatory-review details, and the account rests on unnamed sources rather than company confirmation.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** The Financial Times reported that SpaceX and Tesla have entrusted five directors with negotiating the terms of a potential $3.5tn combination and are racing to complete a deal before the end of the year. Elon Musk said in October that the two companies were exploring a merger, the New York Times reported.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The $3.5tn valuation, the all-stock structure, the assertion that all five directors have ties to Musk and the year-end deadline all rest on unnamed sources; neither company has confirmed a signed agreement, a board vote or a regulatory filing. The FT account describes a single group of five directors working out terms, while the headline refers to two special committees of five — leaving unresolved whether five directors are involved in total or five on each committee.

</div>

## Background

A special committee is a board group set up to evaluate a transaction when executives or large shareholders have a conflict of interest, and its credibility depends on the members' independence from the conflicted parties. The Financial Times reports that the SpaceX and Tesla talks are being handled by two such committees involving five directors described as "disinterested," but that all five have ties to Elon Musk — the detail that makes the governance structure central to how the proposed deal would be assessed.

## Why it matters

For Tesla's public shareholders, the decision would rest with five directors the FT describes as having ties to Elon Musk, so the credibility of their independence — not just the $3.5T valuation — becomes the central issue in any approval, and minority investors and listing-rule reviewers are likely to press on how conflicts were handled. The New York Times reported in October that the two companies were already exploring a merger; the change now is that specific all-stock terms and a named negotiating structure are on the table. If a deal proceeds on these terms, Tesla holders would end up owning stock in a combined company whose center of gravity is Musk's private rocket and satellite business rather than the carmaker.
