<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

Researchers at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (HKUST) reported a lab-scale perovskite solar cell that reached 27.04% power conversion efficiency, with a certified value of 26.67% from an undisclosed certification body. Their trans-interface engineering approach replaces a planar interlayer with vertically aligned, covalently bonded nanolinks that penetrate both the perovskite absorber and the charge-transport layer, intended to improve charge transfer and resist mechanical failure at that interface.

In one-sun maximum-power-point testing, 92% of tested devices retained 80% of initial efficiency for at least 1,400 hours (T80), a result the team presents as evidence of both stability and device-to-device reproducibility. The work was published in Nature Photonics and remains a research result, not a commercial module or independently scaled manufacturing process.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** HKUST researchers published the work in Nature Photonics as the November 2026 cover article (Volume 20, Issue 11), and both the paper's abstract and the HKUST announcement confirm a certified power conversion efficiency of 26.67%, with the team's best cell reaching 27.04%; the paper also reports that 92% of tested devices held T80 lifetimes of at least 1,400 hours under maximum-power-point tracking and one-sun illumination.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The certification body is not named in either the paper abstract or the pv magazine report, the 27.04% figure is the team's own best-cell measurement rather than the certified value, and nothing in the available reporting shows independent replication, module-scale or commercial manufacturing data, or a cost and production timeline — the paper's claim that TIE extends to other multilayer optoelectronic devices also remains untested outside this study.

</div>

## Background

Perovskite solar cells use a thin crystalline absorber that has risen fast in laboratory efficiency but still degrades under heat, moisture and mechanical stress, which is why stability rather than raw power output has held the technology back from mass production. The boundary between the perovskite absorber and the adjacent charge-transport layer is a known weak spot: the usual fix is to insert a flat passivating interlayer, which can improve defect passivation and adhesion but forces a trade-off between extracting charge efficiently and keeping the interface mechanically stable. HKUST's trans-interface engineering instead grows nanolinks that run vertically through both layers, bonding them into one continuous structure rather than placing a separate film between them.

## Why it matters

For perovskite developers, the result points to interface mechanics as well as electrical passivation as a reproducibility lever: HKUST bonds the absorber and transport layer together, and reports that 92% of cells reached the 1,400-hour T80 mark. Teams seeking to compare or reproduce the 26.67% certified figure will need the certification body named and the linker and AIBN-derived radical initiation steps replicated.

## What to watch

Watch for the certification body to be named and for independent groups to reproduce the 26.67% certified result; a module-scale demonstration would be the next test beyond this cell-level paper.
