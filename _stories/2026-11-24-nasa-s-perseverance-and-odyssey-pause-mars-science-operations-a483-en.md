<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

NASA's Perseverance rover and Odyssey orbiter have suspended science operations after separate technical problems, leaving two of the agency's four active Mars missions offline. Perseverance's fault is in its communications system: the rover has two redundant UHF radios for high-rate relay through Mars orbiters and a separate, slower X-band system for direct-to-Earth links, while NASA has not detailed Odyssey's issue.

Both spacecraft are operating beyond their design lifetimes—Perseverance is in its sixth year on Mars and Odyssey has been in orbit since 2001—and NASA described the outages as hopefully temporary.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** NASA's Nov. 19 blog said teams are investigating separate technical issues affecting science operations on the Perseverance rover and the 2001 Mars Odyssey orbiter; Space.com reported that Perseverance has an antenna problem and Odyssey a reaction-wheel problem, and NASA says both are operating beyond their design lifetimes.
- **Not yet confirmed:** How long the suspensions will last, what caused them, and when science work will resume; NASA has not said either spacecraft is lost, so the “knocked offline” framing is broader than the agency's statement, and no independent assessment of the hardware's condition has been published.

</div>

## Background

Mars spacecraft rarely send high-rate data straight to Earth. Perseverance routes most of its traffic over two redundant UHF radios to a fleet of orbiting relays — Odyssey among them — which forward it home, keeping a slower X-band direct-to-Earth link in reserve. Odyssey has been at Mars since 2001 and is the longest-lived Mars mission in history, and Perseverance is in its sixth year on the surface; both are operating past their original design lifetimes, and together with two other missions they make up NASA's four active Mars operations.

## Why it matters

Perseverance's pause interrupts its study of a crater rim that once held an ancient lake, and the communications fault affects the high-rate UHF path it uses to return data through orbiters; the slower X-band direct-to-Earth link remains available as a fallback.
