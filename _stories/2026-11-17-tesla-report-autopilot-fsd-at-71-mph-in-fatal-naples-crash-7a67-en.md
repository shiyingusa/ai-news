<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

Tesla's report to federal regulators states that a Model 3 with Autopilot or Full Self-Driving engaged struck an overturned box truck on I-75 near Naples, Florida, at 71 mph, killing two occupants and a man who had stopped to help. Local police and media covered the May 31 crash as a truck wreck in the dark and did not mention a driver-assist system, according to Electrek, which says the case is part of its investigation into fatal crashes recorded in Tesla's NHTSA data. The disclosure is Tesla's own account to regulators, not an independent determination of what caused the crash. Autopilot and Full Self-Driving are Tesla's branded driver-assistance systems, and Tesla's reports to federal regulators record crashes in which either was engaged.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** Electrek, citing Tesla's report to federal regulators, says Autopilot or Full Self-Driving was engaged at 71 mph when a Model 3 struck an overturned box truck on I-75 near Naples, Florida, on May 31, 2026; MySuncoast reported that day that three people were killed and four seriously injured in the crash (tool-2-1, tool-2-2).
- **Not yet confirmed:** Tesla's filing does not by itself establish that the driver-assist system caused the crash, and no regulator finding is in the supplied material; the 71 mph figure and engaged state are Tesla's own account as relayed by Electrek, not an independent reconstruction. The sources also conflict on whether the man who stopped to help was killed (Electrek) or among the four seriously injured (MySuncoast), while the 2023 model year, five occupants, and Freightliner/BMW details rest on Teslabriefing's account (tool-2-3).

</div>

## Background

NHTSA's Standing General Order requires automakers and operators to report crashes involving automated driving systems or SAE Level 2 driver assistance, such as Tesla's Autopilot and FSD, when the system was engaged within 30 seconds of impact and someone was killed or injured. That filing duty is why Tesla's own report to the agency, rather than the police and local coverage that treated the May 31 crash as a nighttime truck wreck, is what ties the Naples collision to a driver-assist system. Under the same order Tesla has withheld crash narratives, software versions and the system's operating area as confidential business information, leaving the engagement flag as the main public record of what the car was doing.

## Why it matters

For Tesla owners and crash investigators, the case shows that a crash can appear in Tesla's regulatory filing as an Autopilot or FSD event even when local police and media accounts do not mention driver assistance; the system's status must be checked against the filing, not inferred from first coverage.

## What to watch

Watch NHTSA's public ADAS crash data for updates to the Naples case and for any defect investigation into Autopilot or FSD tied to this crash.
