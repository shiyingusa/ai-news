<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

An MIT team led by McGovern Institute investigator Evelina Fedorenko and lead author Andrea Gregor de Varda built "encoding models" — tools that take a multilingual language model's internal numeric representation of a sentence and predict how a person's brain will respond to the same input — for 21 languages, and reported in Nature Neuroscience that the models accurately predicted brain responses in all of them, including understudied languages such as Marathi and Lithuanian. Participants listened to stories from "Alice in Wonderland" in their native language while their brain activity was recorded; 12 languages came from existing lab data and nine (Italian, Portuguese, German, Russian, Polish, Hindi, Arabic, Mandarin, Korean) were newly recruited.

The key result: a model trained on one language could predict brain activity in a language it had never seen — a French-trained model also predicted Vietnamese responses — which the authors say is only possible if speakers represent language in a shared way. Dissecting linguistic features suggested that shared component relates to meaning rather than surface form.

The paper describes this as the first study to use multilingual models to predict brain responses across many languages; the clinical and language-preservation uses the authors mention are future possibilities, not demonstrated applications.

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** MIT News reports that a study led by Andrea Gregor de Varda and senior author Evelina Fedorenko, both of MIT's McGovern Institute, was published in Nature Neuroscience and built encoding models — tools that map a language model's internal numbers onto predicted brain activity — for 21 languages. The team combined existing brain-scan data from speakers of 12 languages with newly recorded data from speakers of nine more (including Italian, German, Russian, Hindi, Arabic, Mandarin and Korean) who listened to "Alice in Wonderland" in their native language, and reports that the models predicted brain responses in every language tested.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The headline interpretation — that the shared component across languages is specifically meaning — rests on the authors' own feature analysis, and the MIT account gives no prediction-accuracy figures, no comparison against alternative explanations, and no independent replication; the version of the text provided also cuts off mid-sentence in de Varda's remarks on how languages differ. The claimed future applications, from multilingual tools for detecting cognitive decline to reconstructing a dying language from one speaker's brain scans, are stated as possibilities, not demonstrated results.

</div>

## Background

Encoding models are the tool behind this work: they take the internal numerical representations a language model produces for a sentence and learn to predict the corresponding brain activity, measured while a person listens to speech. Before this study, such models existed for only a handful of well-studied languages, including English, French and Chinese. Multilingual language models — which process hundreds of languages within one shared representational space — gave the MIT team a single numerical code that could be aligned against brain data from many languages at once, making a 21-language comparison feasible.

## Why it matters

Encoding models previously existed for only a handful of well-studied languages such as English, French and Chinese, and the clinical work that uses AI language tools — for example, detecting cognitive decline from brain-activity patterns during language — has been concentrated in those languages. Because a model trained on one language could predict responses in an unseen one, researchers may need far less per-language brain-scan data to extend such tools to speakers of less-studied languages.
