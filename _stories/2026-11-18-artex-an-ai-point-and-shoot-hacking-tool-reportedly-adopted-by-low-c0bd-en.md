<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

WIRED reports that Artex, an AI-driven penetration-testing tool, lets people with little technical background scan target systems for vulnerabilities and carry out attacks by feeding prompts to a chatbot. The magazine describes it as one of a set of "point-and-shoot" hacking tools that inexperienced criminals are reportedly adopting.

The account does not name the underlying AI model or the tool's makers, give a release date or price, or measure how reliably the attacks succeed.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** WIRED's report describes Artex as an AI-powered penetration-testing tool that inexperienced users can operate through chatbot prompts to scan for and attack vulnerabilities.
- **Not yet confirmed:** that criminals are adopting it at any scale, how effective its attacks actually are, which AI model powers it, who makes it, and what it costs or where it is sold — all of that rests on a single outlet's reporting with no independent testing.

</div>

## Background

Artex is an open-source, LLM-driven "agentic" platform: software that chains a language model to ordinary intrusion tooling so it can automate multiple phases of an attack, from reconnaissance through data exfiltration, according to an advisory posted on the FBI's IC3 portal. Such penetration-testing tools are normally legitimate security software for probing your own systems; the risk is that the same automation works against anyone. AI Daily Radar's November 16 digest reported the FBI's warning that Artex had been used to breach networks in under three hours, citing a U.S. IT company and South Korea's financial sector, but that account did not describe how the tool works — the gap WIRED's report fills by describing prompt-driven use by inexperienced attackers.

## Why it matters

If a chatbot prompt is enough to both find and exploit a vulnerability, security teams can no longer assume that probes against their systems come from skilled operators, and triage rules tuned to expert tradecraft may misfire on crude, high-volume attempts. WIRED gives no figures on how many such attempts exist or how often they work, so the change is in who can launch an attack, not yet in a measured rise in attacks.
