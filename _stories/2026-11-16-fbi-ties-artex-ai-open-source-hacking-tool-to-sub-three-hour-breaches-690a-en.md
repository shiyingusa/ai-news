The FBI says an open-source hacking tool called Artex AI was used to breach networks in under three hours, according to a TechCrunch report on the bureau’s warning. The reported intrusions targeted a U.S. IT company and South Korea’s financial sector.

The account is brief and does not name the victims, describe the tool’s capabilities, or link to the underlying FBI advisory, so the speed claim and attribution remain unverified outside the report.

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** Multiple outlets, citing an FBI warning, report that the bureau named ARTEX — described as an open-source, AI-powered penetration-testing framework whose agents automate network attacks — as a tool used in intrusions, including against a U.S. IT company and South Korea's financial sector.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The claim that some intrusions reached privileged access in under three hours rests on the FBI's account as relayed by secondary outlets; the supplied material does not name the victims, give a count of affected organizations, link the tool to the reported ConnectWise-related supply-chain incident, or include the FBI advisory itself or any independent technical analysis.

</div>

## Background

An open-source hacking tool is distributed with publicly available code that anyone can download, modify and reuse, in contrast to commercial penetration-testing products sold under licence and tied to a single vendor. That distinction matters for the FBI's warning: a tool that is not controlled by one company cannot simply be patched or pulled from the market, and its derivatives are hard to enumerate. Defenders typically describe AI-assisted attack tooling by how far it compresses the time from first contact with a target to a working intrusion, which is the measure the sub-three-hour figure refers to.

## Why it matters

For defenders at banks and IT providers, the practical shift is that open-source tooling like ARTEX, paired with LLMs, is now linked to real intrusions and data exfiltration rather than lab demos: reporting on the South Korean campaign describes exposed loan systems and leaked customer data, which creates follow-on phishing and fraud risk for those customers. Because ARTEX is open source, the workflow is cheap to copy, so the sub-three-hour breach timeline the FBI attributes to it is a reason to shorten detection and containment assumptions rather than treat the tool as a one-off.

## What to watch

Watch for the FBI’s underlying advisory or technical indicators that would let defenders verify the sub-three-hour claim and check for Artex AI activity.
