<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

MIT researchers have demonstrated a flapping-wing microrobot that weighs less than a paperclip and carries its own sensors and microcontroller, flying a complex trajectory and landing on a sunflower outside the lab's motion-capture volume, according to a paper published in Science Robotics. The 244-milligram electronics package combines an inertial measurement unit, an optical-flow sensor and a time-of-flight sensor, and the team reports positional errors of only a few centimeters. The robot still receives power from offboard, so untethered outdoor flight and uses such as pollination remain future goals rather than demonstrated capabilities.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** MIT News reports that the team's paper in Science Robotics describes a flapping-wing microrobot carrying a 244-mg onboard electronics package with an inertial measurement unit, optical-flow sensor, time-of-flight sensor and microcontroller; it followed trajectories and landed on a sunflower outside motion capture while still powered offboard, with positional errors of a few centimeters.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The claim that this is the first sensory- and compute-autonomous aerial microrobot rests on the authors; independent replication, untethered outdoor flight, an onboard camera for obstacle avoidance and pollination use are not demonstrated, and this is a research prototype with no product price or availability.

</div>

## Background

MIT's aerial microrobots have until now flown only inside a motion-capture studio: the robot was too light to carry cameras, inertial sensors or a computer, so external cameras tracked it and offboard electronics did the position estimation. The lab's earlier four-wing design, described by MIT in 2025 as weighing less than a paperclip, could hover and maneuver but stayed tethered to that lab infrastructure. The new robot moves the sensing and computation onto the vehicle itself, although electrical power still arrives through an offboard connection.

## Why it matters

For microrobotics researchers, the demonstration sets a concrete payload target: a 244-mg sensing-and-compute package on a vehicle with a 1.9 lift-to-weight ratio, enough to move navigation off the motion-capture rig. The remaining constraint is power; the team says the electronics use only about 6 percent of total power, but batteries and power electronics must still be integrated before outdoor untethered flight.

## What to watch

The team says it is working toward an onboard camera for object recognition and obstacle avoidance, and integrated power electronics and batteries for untethered outdoor flight; watch for the next demonstration or paper reporting either capability, as no date has been announced.
