<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

IBM has told some of its U.S.-based immigrant employees that it is ending visa and green-card sponsorship, and is offering relocation to another country as an alternative to losing their jobs — an option that can come with a steep pay cut, Rest of World reported. The report, published Nov. 23, says IBM made the decision earlier in the month but does not specify how many employees are affected, which IBM business units or U.S. offices are covered, or how large the pay reductions are.

No public statement from IBM on the scope of the change was cited, so the numbers behind it remain unverified.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** IBM is discontinuing U.S. visa and green-card sponsorship for most roles in IBM Consulting, its largest division with more than 150,000 employees, and visa holders in that unit have been told to relocate abroad or leave by year-end, according to multiple outlets (NationWire, HR Briefing, The Times of India) citing IBM's own statement.
- **Not yet confirmed:** IBM has not published how many employees are affected, which roles or countries remain eligible for sponsorship, the size of the pay cuts tied to relocation, or whether relocation is guaranteed; the precise effective date and the exact wording of the year-end deadline rest on outlet reporting rather than a public IBM document.

</div>

## Background

H-1B is a temporary U.S. work visa that requires an employer sponsor, and green-card sponsorship is the employer-backed route to permanent residency — so when a company withdraws sponsorship, affected workers must find another sponsor, change status or leave the country. IBM's change covers most roles in IBM Consulting, its largest division, which employs more than 150,000 people globally, according to Indian Opinion. Nationwire reported that the move comes amid a wider Trump administration crackdown on skilled foreign hiring that is reshaping large technology employers.

## Why it matters

For affected employees, the choice is material: U.S. work authorization is tied to the sponsoring employer, so a worker who loses sponsorship generally has limited time to find a new sponsor or leave the country, and relocated staff are typically paid according to the local market rather than U.S. rates. Employees weighing the offer should establish in writing what their new salary, location and immigration status would be before accepting.
