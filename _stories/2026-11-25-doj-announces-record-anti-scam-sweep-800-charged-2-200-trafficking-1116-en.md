The U.S. Department of Justice said it has charged more than 800 defendants in over 400 cases filed across 85 federal districts and recovered more than $8 billion in intended victim losses, an action it calls the largest anti-scam crackdown in its history. FBI Director Kash Patel separately described Operation Blackout, an interagency effort that he said dismantled 29 fraud compounds in Asia, Africa and the Middle East, seized almost $17 billion in funds and more than 14,000 devices, and freed more than 2,200 trafficked workers; about 50 people face U.S. federal charges in that operation, and foreign partners arrested or detained more than 320 others.

The two money figures are not the same measure: $8 billion is the DOJ's figure for intended victim losses, while $17 billion is the FBI's seizure total for Operation Blackout, and the accounts give no breakdown of how much has been returned to victims. The push follows a March executive order from President Donald Trump requiring a government-wide crackdown on fraud and predatory schemes.

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** The Justice Department's own announcement, dated this week, states that federal prosecutors have charged more than 800 defendants in over 400 cases across 85 federal districts, and that more than $8 billion in intended victim losses was recovered; Attorney General Todd Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel are named as the officials presenting the results, and the DOJ release describes FBI-led Operation Blackout as having restrained nearly $17 billion in illicit funds, seized more than 14,000 digital devices and dismantled 29 scam compounds. Fox News and Yahoo carried the same $8 billion and 800-defendant figures.
- **Not yet confirmed:** No independent audit backs any of the totals, and the two dollar amounts measure different things — roughly $17 billion "restrained" by the FBI versus more than $8 billion in "intended victim losses recovered" from prosecutions — so the headline's framing of a single $17 billion seizure should not be read as money returned to victims. The count of 2,200 trafficked workers freed, the 320-plus arrests by foreign partners and the 50 people facing U.S. charges under Operation Blackout rest solely on FBI statements, and the report does not say how many of the 800 defendants have been convicted versus merely charged.

</div>

## Background

The sweep follows a March executive order by President Donald Trump requiring a government-wide crackdown on fraud and predatory schemes. The "compounds" at the center of Operation Blackout are centralized facilities where, according to the FBI, trafficked workers are forced to run online scams — a model that has made fraud a cross-border, multibillion-dollar business. Tom's Hardware cites Gallup data estimating that Americans lost $68 billion to scams in 2025.

## Why it matters

The enforcement targets the operating infrastructure rather than individual scammers — the 29 dismantled compounds and the more than 2,200 trafficked workers freed point at the coerced-labor pipeline that staffs many online fraud operations, so the disruption is to the labor supply as much as to the money flow. Victims should not read the seizure figures as restitution: the DOJ describes $8 billion as intended losses, and neither the department's account nor the reporting gives a repayment figure, so anyone defrauded by these networks has no announced route to recover funds from the seizures.
