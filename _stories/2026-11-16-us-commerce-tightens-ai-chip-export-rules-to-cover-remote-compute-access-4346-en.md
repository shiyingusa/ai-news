<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

The US Commerce Department issued an interim rule that requires firms to obtain export licenses for AI chips when the hardware may provide compute to China or other restricted countries, extending controls beyond physical shipments to remote access. Bloomberg reported that the rule specifically targets remote access to some AI chips and servers from Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil.

The available source excerpts do not specify the rule's effective date, the license criteria, or whether a public comment period applies.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** The U.S. Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim rule on Monday requiring licenses to export advanced AI chips to firms headquartered in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, according to Bloomberg, CBS News and Business Insider, all citing the department's action. The stated purpose is to stop companies in those countries from giving Chinese firms remote access to American AI chips and servers.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The specific chips or performance thresholds covered, the rule's effective date, and whether it takes effect immediately or after a comment period — none of these appear in the reporting, and the underlying rule text was not reviewed. The core detail that the measure targets remote access as a workaround rests on the three outlets' reading of the interim rule rather than on the published regulatory text; the source item itself supplies only a one-sentence summary.

</div>

## Background

The rule responds to a gap that earlier reporting had documented: AI Daily Radar's November 15 digest covered a Wall Street Journal investigation finding that Nvidia AI chips were reaching Chinese companies through large Southeast Asian data centers that supplied them with compute while staying technically outside U.S. sanctions. The same day's digest also noted a New York Times report that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had pushed to narrow chip restrictions affecting data centers in the region, some of them clients of his former firm, Cantor Fitzgerald. An interim rule is a measure that takes effect while the agency drafts a final version, so licensing requirements can begin before the usual comment process concludes.

## Why it matters

Cloud providers and data-center operators in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil — markets Chinese firms have used to rent compute — now need to determine whether AI chips they export or make remotely available could serve restricted parties, and to secure a license before doing so. Because the reporting does not spell out the licensing criteria, operators in those countries face legal uncertainty when sizing or selling regional capacity.

## What to watch

Watch for Commerce's full text and effective date for the interim rule, along with any comment period, which will show how broadly the phrase "may provide compute" is interpreted.
