METR, the AI evaluation nonprofit, has published a new version of its widely shared "time horizon" plot, which measures how long a task a model can complete, and reports that some capabilities are still improving exponentially while models perform worse on tasks requiring judgment than on coding. The update adds more open-ended tasks, including designing AI evaluations and scoring philosophical arguments, and the models struggled with them.

MIT Technology Review's The Download newsletter, which summarizes the report, frames that gap as potentially crucial to automating research and development. The newsletter does not link directly to METR's report, so the specific task counts, scores and doubling times behind the update are not given in the account available here.

## Background

METR is a nonprofit that runs its own AI evaluations and publishes the results, and its time-horizon metric measures how long a task — in terms of the time a human expert would need — a model can complete reliably. The earlier version of the plot, described in METR's March 19, 2025 paper and its January 29, 2026 "Time Horizon 1.1" release, characterized the frontier trend as task length doubling roughly every seven months, or about 196 days. MIT Technology Review's February 2026 article had already called it the most misunderstood graph in AI; the new edition keeps that framing but broadens the task suite beyond well-specified work to open-ended judgment tasks.

## Why it matters

Teams that treat time-horizon doubling as a proxy for when models can automate R&D should test on open-ended judgment tasks rather than code-style benchmarks, since METR's update indicates those two capabilities are not advancing at the same rate.

## What to watch

Look for METR's full report and task-level results, and whether the judgment-task gap narrows in its next time-horizon update.
