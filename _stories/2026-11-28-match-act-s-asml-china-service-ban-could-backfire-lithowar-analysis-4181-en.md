<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

A LithoWar analysis argues that the pending US export-control measure known as the MATCH Act could backfire if it bars ASML from servicing the roughly 350 immersion DUV (deep-ultraviolet) scanners already installed in China. Rather than idling those machines, the analysis contends, a service cutoff would open room for rival firms to maintain the tools, for reverse engineering of equipment ASML can no longer support, and for competitors to recruit ASML-trained staff.

The bill targets servicing rather than new sales, and it is a proposed measure, not enacted law; the backfire outcome is the author's argument, not a measured result.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** The MATCH Act is a pending US export-control measure that, as LithoWar describes it, would bar sales of immersion DUV lithography scanners to China and cut off parts, service and software support for machines already installed there; explainx and bits-chips.com describe the bill the same way, with explainx reporting it is currently folded into the Senate's defense bill and could be attached to the FY2027 defense legislation in mid-December 2026. LithoWar attributes the figure of roughly 350 immersion scanners in China to ASML's installed base there, and bits-chips.com likewise reports the bill would cut support for "much of the installed fleet."
- **Not yet confirmed:** The ~350 figure is an estimate that none of the available reports traces to an ASML disclosure or a regulatory document, and LithoWar's central claim — that a servicing ban would let rivals take over maintenance, reverse-engineer the tools and hire ASML's Chinese staff — is the author's argument about unintended consequences, not a measured outcome. Whether the measure passes, and in what final form, remains open; the reports also note Dutch government opposition and ASML CEO Christophe Fouquet's warning that a total cutoff could backfire, but the source material available here is a headline and teaser, so the underlying sourcing for those positions could not be checked.

</div>

## Background

Immersion DUV scanners — ASML's Twinscan NXT line — are the workhorse machines that pattern most chips made on mature and mid-range nodes, and US export controls have so far focused on blocking sales of new tools rather than on the installed base already sitting inside China. Tom's Hardware reported that Chinese fabs have been squeezing more life out of those older immersion DUV tools as controls tightened, while ZeroHedge reported that China had accumulated roughly 340 ASML scanners and that chip hawks in Congress were moving to attach several export-control bills to the must-pass FY2027 defense bill. Because these machines depend on ASML-supplied parts, calibration and field engineers, service access is the remaining lever the MATCH Act would pull — the premise of LithoWar's argument.

## Why it matters

The lever at stake is control over an installed base rather than new sales: if ASML is cut off from servicing those ~350 tools, the analysis says maintenance know-how and spare-part supply could migrate to third-party service providers and Chinese firms, eroding the long-term grip the control is meant to preserve. Fabs running those scanners would then need alternative service and parts arrangements, and ASML would lose the service relationship that keeps its tools tied to its own ecosystem.

## What to watch

Whether the MATCH Act advances from a proposed measure into enacted law, and how the final text defines "servicing" — the treatment of spare parts, on-site engineers and software updates determines whether the backfire scenario is plausible.
