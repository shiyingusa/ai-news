<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

The UK Financial Conduct Authority plans to require decentralized finance (DeFi) platforms — services that run lending, trading and similar functions through smart contracts rather than a central operator — to obtain authorization before serving UK users, according to a Financial Times report. The FCA cited "lack of consumer protections" and the Binance heist, which the report puts at $6.3 billion, as its reasons.

The FT describes the approach as stricter than those taken in the US and the EU. No draft rules, scope definition or timetable were included in the account, so the plan is a stated intention rather than a published or finalized regime.

</div>

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** The Financial Times reported that the FCA says it plans to require DeFi platforms to obtain authorization, and that the regulator gave consumer-protection gaps and the Binance hack as its rationale.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The exact scope (which activities and which entities count as the regulated party), the timing of any consultation or final rules, whether the requirement is a firm policy decision or a proposal, and the $6.3 billion loss figure — none of these are detailed in the available account, and no FCA publication or statement was cited directly.

</div>

## Background

DeFi, or decentralised finance, refers to lending, trading and similar services run by smart contracts on public blockchains rather than by a licensed firm with a legal entity behind it — the gap the FCA cites when it says the sector lacks consumer protections. The case the FT reports the regulator pointing to is the October hack at the centralised exchange Binance, which it described as a record $6.3bn theft; Forklog reported that hackers linked to North Korea laundered the proceeds through decentralised protocols including THORChain. AI Daily Radar's 23 November digest reported that the US Department of Justice was seeking forfeiture of more than $1 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a court request rather than a confirmed recovery, which illustrates how far the aftermath of that heist has already reached into US enforcement.

## Why it matters

If adopted, the requirement would make the UK one of the few major jurisdictions to force DeFi front-ends and protocol operators to hold authorization, which for teams without a legal entity in the UK most likely means geoblocking British users or restructuring how the service is offered — and because most DeFi protocols are permissionless, enforcement would probably fall on websites, wallets and interfaces rather than on the contracts themselves.

## What to watch

Watch for the FCA's formal consultation or policy paper, which should define which DeFi activities fall in scope and when the requirement would take effect.
