<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

A team at MIT, the Whitehead Institute and Charité–Universitätsmedizin Berlin has published in Science (Oct. 29) a single-cell transcriptome atlas covering an entire adult vertebrate: the transparent fish Danionella cerebrum, which is about 1 centimeter long. They profiled more than 600,000 cells from the brain, spinal cord, eyes, internal organs, fins, head and trunk using single-cell RNA sequencing, then paired those profiles with spatial methods to map cell types and gene activity back to their locations in tissue.

The atlas is meant as a molecular reference — which cell types exist, which genes distinguish them, and where they sit — and is publicly browsable through an interactive site called DigiFish. The paper also uses the map to trace how the fish regrows telencephalon neurons after injury, from early wound and immune response through neural stem-cell activity to maturing new neurons.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** The study appears in Science with a DOI, and the authors report profiling more than 600,000 cells across the listed tissues; the atlas is accessible to anyone through the DigiFish site hosted by the Whitehead Institute.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The "first whole-body atlas of an adult vertebrate" framing is the authors' claim about the state of the field, not an independently adjudicated record; the paper states that what the retained positional gene programs actually do in adult fibroblasts remains unknown, and no independent group has yet validated the atlas annotations or the regeneration sequence.

</div>

## Background

A single-cell transcriptome atlas records which genes each individual cell is actively using, read out by sequencing the RNA from thousands of individual cells; such atlases are routine for single organs, but scaling one to a whole adult animal has been blocked by size and cost. Danionella cerebrum, formally described in 2021, is a roughly 1-centimeter transparent fish that is closely related to zebrafish, so genetic tools built for zebrafish largely carry over to it — though it has its own distinct biology rather than being a miniature transparent zebrafish.

## Why it matters

Because Danionella is transparent and can borrow genetic tools developed for zebrafish, labs can use the atlas to pick a specific cell type or gene, then watch it in a living animal — a combination that is hard to assemble in mice or larger vertebrates, where whole-body profiling at this resolution is prohibitively expensive. The team presents the map as a baseline for studying how injury, aging or drugs change cells across the whole body rather than in one dissected organ.
