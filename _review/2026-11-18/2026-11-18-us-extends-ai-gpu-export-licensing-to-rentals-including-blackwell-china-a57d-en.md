<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

The Trump administration extended U.S. export licensing to cover large-scale rentals of high-end AI accelerators, including Nvidia's Blackwell chips, to every country on the D:5 arms-embargo list; sales of such chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now also require a license. Tom's Hardware reports that the change is intended to close the loophole of renting restricted GPUs instead of buying them. China's Ministry of Commerce responded that it "will take countermeasures to firmly safeguard national sovereignty, security, and development interests" ahead of a scheduled Trump–Xi meeting on Thursday, Nov. 19. The article does not define "large quantities" or give the rule's effective date.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** TechCrunch reported on Nov. 16, 2026 that the U.S. issued an interim rule requiring firms to obtain licenses to export AI chips that may provide compute to China or other restricted countries, and China's Ministry of Commerce published a statement, cited by Tom's Hardware, saying China "will take countermeasures to firmly safeguard national sovereignty, security, and development interests."
- **Not yet confirmed:** the rule's exact reach — which accelerators beyond Nvidia's Blackwell line are covered, what volume counts as "large quantities," and whether sales to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil in fact now require licenses, all of which rest on the article's reading rather than a cited regulatory text; the timing is also inconsistent, with the source saying the extension came "yesterday" (Nov 17) while TechCrunch dated it Nov 16, and a CNBC report excerpt describes "impending legislation" rather than a Commerce interim rule. China has not specified any countermeasure, the Xi–Jinping meeting slated for Thursday and its outcome are unconfirmed, and the article's claims about Jensen Huang and Alice Huang are reported allegations, not established findings.

</div>

## Background

Country Group D:5 is the Export Administration Regulations' list of arms-embargoed destinations, so tying rental licences to that list pulls in far more than the handful of countries named in earlier drafts of the policy. AI Daily Radar's November 16 digest reported that Commerce's interim rule already required licences when AI chips could supply compute to China or other restricted countries, naming Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil; the regulation described here extends the licensing requirement to large-scale GPU rentals across the entire D:5 list. The November 17 digest covered Nvidia privately warning customers that the still-unenacted Remote Access Security Act could expose them to US legal action — this report does not say whether that bill has since become law.

## Why it matters

Cloud providers, data-center operators and GPU brokers will need to screen rental customers' identity, end use and destination before serving D:5 markets, adding licensing delay and compliance exposure to deals that previously fell outside sale-based controls. The rule also introduces license checks for chip shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which the article says Commerce suspects are used to route Blackwell cards to China.

## What to watch

Watch the Trump–Xi meeting on Thursday, Nov. 19, for whether Beijing specifies its countermeasures, and the Jan. 10, 2027 expiry of China's suspension of new rare-earth export controls, which the article identifies as leverage if the truce unravels.
