An "instability" in the complex US aviation communications system set off an FAA ground stop that produced a chaotic few hours for air traffic controllers and pilots, Wired reported. Nearly a week after the November 13 disruption, officials still did not fully know what happened, according to the report. The account describes the episode — in the words of one participant, "the hairiest 90 minutes of my career" — as rooted in the communications systems controllers and pilots rely on rather than in an individual aircraft or crew. Wired's reporting does not identify the specific system component that failed, the geographic scope of the stop, or how many flights were affected.

</div>

<div class="fact-check" markdown="1">

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** The FAA issued a nationwide ground stop on Nov. 13, 2026, after what the agency described as a telecommunications failure that triggered network data flooding and instability across air traffic facilities; the disruption began around 3:30 p.m. Eastern and affected 17 of 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers, according to the FAA. The agency says operators canceled roughly 12,462 flights that day, including 9,082 by major airlines, and that all flights landed safely, though it notes not all cancellations were directly caused by the ground stop.
- **Not yet confirmed:** The precise root cause — CNBC reported that engineers have reproduced the network problem on a router, but the FAA has not publicly identified a definitive fault or said whether other routers in the national backbone share it, and roughly a week later officials still did not fully know what happened.

</div>

## Background

A ground stop is an air traffic control measure that holds aircraft on the ground at their departure airports, typically to keep traffic from overwhelming a specific facility or region; unlike a full grounding, flights already airborne are usually allowed to continue. Aviation communications systems are the channels controllers and pilots rely on to relay clearances and instructions, so instability there forces controllers to slow or halt traffic as a precaution. This account describes a chaotic few hours for controllers and pilots, with the cause still unresolved almost a week later.

## Why it matters

Because officials had not established a root cause nearly a week on, the same instability could recur without warning, and the operational lever available is blunt: halting or slowing traffic. Controllers, pilots and airlines should treat short-notice ground stops as a live possibility until the FAA names the failing component and says what changed, since the communications link involved is used in routine operations, not only in emergencies.

## What to watch

Watch for the FAA's root-cause finding or public explanation of which communications system was unstable and whether procedures or backup paths were changed. Any subsequent ground stop of similar origin would indicate the underlying problem was not resolved.
