Amani Al-Khatahtbeh, a Muslim author, is suing xAI after people used its Grok chatbot to generate altered images of her with her hijab removed, Wired reported. Her lawyer, Hillary Nappi, framed litigation as the lever available to individuals, telling Wired: "The only way you get some change against Big Tech is to sue them." The report does not detail the specific legal claims, the damages sought, or when the suit was filed, and xAI has not publicly responded in the account.

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** Amani Al-Khatahtbeh, founder of Muslim Girl, has sued xAI, alleging that Grok users prompted the tool to remove her hijab and depict her in lingerie and a bikini; per Wired's reporting and secondary write-ups of the filing, the complaint raises claims including nonconsensual distribution of sexual images, design defects, negligence and emotional distress, and seeks up to $250,000 under the California Civil Code. The complaint also asserts that xAI could and should have designed Grok to block prompts that remove religious garments such as the hijab from images.
- **Not yet confirmed:** No statement from xAI appears in the available reports, so the company's account of Grok's safeguards is absent. The case number, court and filing date, and the precise statutory basis for the $250,000 figure, are drawn only from secondary summaries rather than the complaint itself, and no independent testing has shown how Grok handles such prompts or how widely the images circulated.

</div>

## Background

Amani Al-Khatahtbeh is the founder of MuslimGirl.com, a media outlet focused on Muslim women in America. According to Wired, she posted a selfie on January 1 from Zohran Mamdani’s inauguration as New York City’s first Muslim mayor, and replies soon included images altered with xAI’s Grok chatbot to remove her hijab. Her lawyer, Hillary Nappi, told Wired that “the only way you get some change against Big Tech is to sue them,” framing the case as a challenge to platform liability.

## Why it matters

The case targets the image-editing capability of a major chatbot rather than the person who made the images, testing whether an AI developer can be held responsible for nonconsensual altered depictions produced with its tool. For teams shipping image generation or editing features, that puts the design of abuse filters and identity-related safeguards on the same footing as the model itself, since the litigation risk attaches to what the product permits, not only to what users do.

## What to watch

Watch for xAI's formal response to the complaint and any ruling on an early dismissal motion, which would show whether the court treats a developer's image tooling as a basis for liability.
