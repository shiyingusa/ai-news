<div class="story-lead" markdown="1">

Prosecutors told a federal judge that they found hundreds of audio files on the personal computer of Abdulrahman Alfozan, a former OpenAI employee accused of hiding a recording device behind the company's executive conference-room television, Bloomberg reported on November 23. Dozens of the files are recordings of OpenAI meetings and conversations from September 2026, many involving what a court filing describes as "what may be trade secrets," and Alfozan kept AI-generated transcripts alongside them, uploading some audio to Dropbox and renaming files by content. The filing also says he was in frequent communication with people affiliated with the Saudi government while recording; his lawyer says he has no special or covert ties to Riyadh, and the October 27 indictment charges him with interception of oral communications, not trade-secret theft or economic espionage. Prosecutors are urging the judge to deny bail, arguing his weak US ties and Saudi connections create flight risk, and a hearing is set for Tuesday.

## Verified and unconfirmed

- **Verified:** A US indictment dated October 27 charges former OpenAI employee Abdulrahman Alfozan with interception of oral communications — not trade-secret theft or economic espionage — and Bloomberg reported on November 6 that he pleaded not guilty; he is a Saudi native and US green-card holder. Prosecutors' filings, as reported by Bloomberg and cited by Tom's Hardware, say hundreds of audio files were found on his personal computer, including dozens of recordings of OpenAI meetings and conversations from September 2026, some with AI-generated transcripts, with files also uploaded to Dropbox and images of a confidential "strategic partnership" document found on his phone.
- **Not yet confirmed:** That any material actually constituted trade secrets, that it was shared with or intended for the Saudi government, or that national security was harmed — Alfozan denies taking secrets for Saudi Arabia and his lawyer says he has no covert government ties, so prosecutors' characterization rests on their own filing. The sources also conflict on his custody status: the Tom's Hardware account says a judge could deny his $1 million bond request at a Tuesday hearing, while CNBC reported on November 24 that he was ordered released; the recordings' contents and recipients remain under investigation, and further charges may follow.

</div>

## Background

The case began in October 2026, when OpenAI found a covert recorder hidden behind the television in an executive conference room — a device built on a Seeed Xiao ESP32-S3, a matchbook-sized hobbyist microcontroller board costing roughly $14 that was intended to capture sensitive conversations. The device was discovered days later, and investigators linked it to Abdulrahman Alfozan, leading to his arrest and removal from the company. A October 27 indictment charged him with interception of oral communications rather than theft of trade secrets or economic espionage, a distinction that frames the current dispute over whether he should be released on bail.

## Why it matters

A device built on a cheap Seeed Xiao ESP32-S3 microcontroller, reportedly costing about $14, got past whatever network, endpoint and access controls OpenAI runs — a reminder for AI labs and other research-heavy firms that physical sweeps of sensitive meeting rooms are a separate control from digital security. The charging decision also carries weight for deterrence: prosecutors have brought only an interception count so far, and trade-secret or economic-espionage charges would hinge on what investigators establish about where the recordings went.

## What to watch

The Tuesday bail hearing, where the judge will rule on Alfozan's request for a $1 million bond, and whether prosecutors add trade-secret or espionage charges as they determine what he did or planned to do with the recordings.
